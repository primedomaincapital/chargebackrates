Chargeback Prevention ROI: What the Data Says

Alert networks, representment services, and pre-dispute resolution each promise to shrink dispute losses. But prevention spending is itself a cost — and the industry has been slow to measure whether the stack earns its keep.

Prime Domain Capital  ·  October 2026

A merchant's dispute stack has three layers. Prevention tries to stop disputes before they are filed: alert networks that warn of an impending chargeback so the merchant can refund first, and pre-dispute programs run by the card networks that resolve cardholder inquiries before they become formal disputes. Representment fights disputes that do get filed, assembling evidence to win reversals. Analytics — the thinnest layer — is supposed to tell the merchant whether the first two are working.

Each layer has a price. Alert networks charge per alert. Representment providers take a fee per dispute, win or lose. Pre-dispute enrollment carries its own costs. For a merchant processing significant volume, the annual dispute-stack spend can rival the direct losses it is meant to prevent — which makes ROI measurement not an academic exercise but a procurement decision.

“You cannot manage what you do not benchmark — and the dispute industry has never published its benchmarks.”

How the math is supposed to work

Alerts convert would-be chargebacks into refunds. The merchant still loses the sale, but avoids the dispute fee, the representment cost, and — critically — the increment to their dispute ratio. For merchants near network monitoring thresholds, a single avoided dispute can be worth far more than its face value.

Representment recovers revenue on disputes already filed. The honest accounting subtracts the provider's per-dispute fee and the internal cost of evidence assembly from every win — and counts losses net of fees paid. Merchants that track this rigorously often discover their “win rate” overstates their net recovery by a wide margin.

Pre-dispute programs sit between the two: they intercept cardholder inquiries through issuer collaboration before a dispute code is assigned. The value proposition is structural — disputes that never happen never touch the ratio — but measurement requires comparing dispute volumes against a counterfactual, which demands exactly the kind of industry benchmarking data that does not publicly exist.

The missing denominator

Every ROI calculation above needs a baseline: what do merchants like me lose to disputes, per thousand transactions, in my vertical? Without it, vendors sell on anecdotes and merchants buy on fear. A public, independent chargeback rates index — dispute incidence by industry, alert conversion rates, representment net-recovery by reason code — would give both sides a shared denominator.

The company that publishes that index becomes the arbiter of the industry's economics. Vendors will cite it. Merchants will budget against it. Journalists will quote it. And the name on the masthead will be the category itself: ChargebackRates.com.
