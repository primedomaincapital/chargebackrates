Chargeback Fees and Statistics 2026: What a Dispute Actually Costs

A single chargeback costs $128 on average before the sale itself is counted. Visa charges the loser $600 to argue about it. The fee schedule is public — the bill is not.

Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

The dispute was over a $47 phone case. The merchant won it — and still lost money. The processor kept its $15 dispute fee, the representment provider billed its per-case charge, and two staff hours went into evidence the issuer never read. That is the arithmetic this market runs on: the fee schedule is published, the bill is assembled later, and the merchant pays both.

The fee stack, in order

Start with the processor, because its fee lands first and applies win or lose. Stripe charges $15 per dispute in the US, plus a $15 counter fee when the merchant contests it — the counter fee comes back if the merchant wins, the $15 does not. PayPal charges $20 per card chargeback. Shopify Payments charges $15, returned when the merchant wins. Square charges nothing. Those are the published fee pages, as compiled in November 2026 — and they are the smallest numbers in this article, which is exactly why merchants underestimate them: a fee that small, applied to every dispute, is a tax on volume.

Representment is where the bill grows. Fighting a dispute means pulling order records, delivery confirmations, IP logs, and customer messages, then formatting them to the network’s evidence standards — and Visa’s Compelling Evidence 3.0 framework raised the bar on what counts. Mastercard’s 2026 research with Javelin puts the all-in cost at $128 per chargeback: $82 of internal staff and systems cost plus $46 of third-party fees. For merchants with more than $1 billion in revenue, the internal cost alone runs to $151.88 per dispute — nearly double the all-merchant average. The reversed sale is the smallest part of the loss. The work of contesting it is the larger one.

The amount at stake varies by what the merchant sells. Mastercard’s 2025 data with Datos Insights puts the average disputed amount at $120 in travel and hospitality, $99 in high-risk categories, $84 in retail, $77 in digital goods, and $69 in subscription services. The United States averages $110 across industries, against $94 in Brazil, $91 in Australia, and $82 in the United Kingdom. A subscription merchant and a travel merchant are not fighting the same dispute, even when the fee schedule says they are.

Arbitration: the $600 question

When representment fails, the case can go one step further: pre-arbitration, where the issuer and acquirer try to settle, then arbitration, where the card network rules and the decision is binding. Arbitration is expensive by design. Visa charges $600 to whichever side loses — raised from $500 on April 1, 2025. Mastercard’s losing-party fee is $575, built from a $25 Mastercom fee, a $200 filing fee, and a $350 administration fee. A missed pre-arbitration deadline costs about $15; Mastercard can add a $100 technical-violation fee, and withdrawing a filed case runs $150 to $320. The figures come from Adyen’s published Visa and Mastercard chargeback guidelines, as summarized by Chargeflow in November 2026. Older guides still show $500 and $400 — the pre-2025 numbers — so confirm the current fee with your acquirer before relying on either. One more constraint: Stripe does not support the arbitration phase at all, so Stripe merchants cannot go past representment.

The fee changes the decision, not just the cost. A $600 loser-pays fee on a $47 dispute is not a close call; it is a refund. Arbitration belongs to high-value disputes with strong evidence, and even there the odds are poor — outcomes at this stage tend to favor issuers over merchants, and a decision takes about 45 days at Visa and 60 to 90 days at Mastercard. A merchant who escalates a weak case pays twice: once for the dispute, once for the privilege of losing it.

The thresholds that turn fees into fines

None of the fees above is the real danger. The real danger is the ratio. Under Visa’s VAMP program, a merchant crosses into the Above Standard tier at a 0.9% combined fraud-and-dispute ratio, and into the Excessive tier at 1.5% — the excessive line was cut from 2.2% on April 1, 2026 — with at least 1,500 combined fraud reports and disputes in the month. Acquirers face their own tiers at 0.5% (Above Standard) and 0.7% (Excessive). Visa charges $4 per flagged transaction in the Above Standard tier and $8 in the Excessive tier. Mastercard’s Excessive Chargeback Merchant program triggers at a 1.5% ratio with 100 or more chargebacks in a month; the High-Excessive tier starts at 3.0% with 300 or more. Cross the line and the fees stop being per-dispute and start being monthly — monitoring programs, fines, and in the extreme, the loss of the ability to accept cards at all.

That is why the ratio is measured before the dollars. A merchant can survive losing disputes. Losing the ability to take payment is existential, and the ratio decides which of those two problems a merchant has.

Fraud, by the numbers

The global bill keeps growing. The Nilson Report projects global card fraud losses of $41.06 billion by 2030, on $70.73 trillion of card volume. Datos Insights, via Mastercard, puts the global financial impact of chargebacks at $36.9 billion in 2026, rising to $46.1 billion by 2029. And the fastest-growing slice of it is the hardest to fight: friendly fraud — first-party misuse by legitimate cardholders. Chargebacks911’s 2026 Chargeback Field Report, built from more than 250 merchants, found 83.4% of enterprise merchants saw friendly fraud in the period, and that once second-cycle chargebacks are counted, merchants recover just 10.7% of what fraud costs them. Nine dollars in ten are gone before the dispute is even filed.

The honest reading of those numbers is uncomfortable for both sides of the vendor market. Prevention sellers quote the $36.9 billion as proof every merchant needs their product. The data says something narrower: most of the loss is fraud the merchant never sees coming, and the recovery rate on what is seen is barely one in ten. A vendor that cannot move the recovery number is selling activity, not results.

The average rate that does not exist

Search for the average e-commerce chargeback rate and you will find plenty of answers. None of them is reliable. The card networks do not publish industry-level chargeback rates, and the vendor tables that claim to fill the gap use incompatible definitions — different denominators, different dispute stages, different verticals — so they cannot be compared with each other, let alone averaged. This site’s market-data page excludes those tables for exactly that reason. What a merchant can measure, honestly, is its own ratio against the network thresholds above, and its own dispute incidence per thousand transactions in its own vertical. A benchmark that cannot be defined cannot be beaten, and a vendor selling against one is selling a number it invented.

There is a second reason the averages mislead. At $128 of all-in cost per dispute, contesting a $20 subscription chargeback is a loss even when the merchant wins: the transaction value comes back, the $15 processor fee and the evidence labor do not. The fight-or-refund line is not a matter of principle. It is arithmetic, and it moves with ticket size — which is why the merchants who manage disputes best decide by amount, by reason code, and by evidence strength, not by habit.

The dispute business rewards the merchant who does three unglamorous things: watches the ratio weekly, prices the full stack before signing with any vendor, and treats arbitration as a separate decision with its own fee, not as the next step in a fight already lost. The fee schedule is public. The bill is not. The merchants who read both are the ones who still have a margin when the month ends.
