Chargebacks911 Pricing: What the Enterprise Chargeback Service Actually Costs

Chargebacks911 does not publish a price. Enterprise merchants report custom quotes starting around $1,000 a month — and a service that only makes sense above a certain volume of disputes.

Prime Domain Capital · November 2026

The quote arrived after the sales call, because it always does. A finance director at a midsize e-commerce merchant — about 400 chargebacks a month, a dispute ratio that had crept past 1% — had spent three weeks trying to find out what Chargebacks911 charges. The website offered case studies, win-rate claims, and a form to request a consultation. It did not offer a price. When the number finally came, it was a range, a minimum, and a contract term, and none of it was in writing until the order form. That is the Chargebacks911 pricing problem in miniature: the service is sold like enterprise software, priced like a retainer, and measured by a win rate nobody has agreed how to define.

The price nobody publishes

Chargebacks911 does not publish a price. The company quotes privately, by solution, integration, and volume, and the quote requires a sales discovery call and usually an annual commitment before a single dispute is submitted. Outside estimates published in 2026 comparison guides put enterprise contracts at $1,000 to $3,000 a month, with a minimum monthly commitment reported between $500 and $2,000 — and the consistent finding across every guide is that the number is a floor, not a fee schedule. One comparison listing shows a $99 starting figure; the enterprise contracts the guides actually document start around $1,000 a month. The gap between those two numbers is the gap between a listing and a contract, and merchants who budget on the listing learn the difference in the first invoice.

The structure matters more than the sticker. Chargebacks911 is a managed service, not software: dedicated account management, fraud analytics, and representment handled by the vendor’s team. That is why the price is a monthly commitment rather than a per-case fee, and why the company typically works with merchants above roughly 100 disputes a month. Below that volume, the fixed monthly cost is the whole story — $1,000 a month to manage 40 disputes is $25 a dispute before a single fee is counted, and most of those disputes are worth less than the cost of fighting them.

What the money buys

What the retainer buys is scale and coverage. Chargebacks911, founded in 2011, is the enterprise chargeback manager the 2026 guides compare everyone else against: integrations across most major processors, a reported win rate around 80% on managed cases — the highest the guides report — and a research arm whose 2026 Chargeback Field Report, built from more than 250 merchants, is the source behind the industry’s most quoted friendly-fraud figures. The report found 83.4% of enterprise merchants saw friendly fraud in the period, and that once second-cycle chargebacks are counted, merchants recover just 10.7% of what fraud costs them. Those are the numbers that sell the service: the problem is large, the recovery is small, and somebody has to do the work.

The honest version of the pitch is narrower than the marketing. An 80% win rate on managed cases is a strong number, but it is a rate on the cases the vendor chooses to fight, measured by the vendor’s own definition of a win. The merchant’s number — the share of disputes closed in favor of the merchant, net of every fee — is lower, and it is the only one that pays the rent. A vendor that wins 80% of the cases it takes and takes the easiest 50% of the caseload is not an 80% vendor. It is a vendor with a good filter.

The number that should worry you

There is a second reason to read the win rate carefully. In November 2023, Chargebacks911 settled an FTC and Florida enforcement action over misleading win-rate claims in its marketing — the regulator’s version of the same warning this article is giving. The settlement did not shut the company down, and it did not end the enterprise business. It did put a permanent asterisk next to the headline number, and any merchant evaluating the service should ask for the win rate defined in writing: which cases count, what counts as a win, and what the net recovery is after fees. If the answer comes with a definition, the vendor is serious. If it comes with a brochure, it is not.

When it is worth it — and when it is not

The arithmetic is simple enough to do on the quote itself. At 500 disputes a month and a $2,000 monthly commitment, the fixed cost is $4 a dispute — trivial against the average transaction value. At 100 disputes a month and a $1,000 minimum, it is $10 a dispute before representment fees, and the cases worth fighting are the minority. The 2026 guides agree on the shape of the answer even where they disagree on the numbers: under 200 to 300 disputes a month, the fixed monthly cost usually exceeds the value of the disputes being managed, and the merchant is better served by a success-fee model — Chargeflow at 15 to 25% of recovered revenue, or per-alert prevention tools at $15 to $29 an alert with no monthly minimum — than by an enterprise retainer.

Above that line, the retainer starts to make sense, and the buying question changes. It is no longer whether the service is worth the money. It is whether the vendor’s definition of winning matches yours, whether the contract lets you leave if it doesn’t, and whether the monthly minimum is a floor you will clear in a slow month. Get the quote in writing, with the win-rate definition attached. If they will not define the win rate, the price does not matter — you are not buying a service, you are buying a number, and the number is theirs.
