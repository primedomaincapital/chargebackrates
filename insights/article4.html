Friendly Fraud: The $100 Billion Problem Merchants Misdiagnose

Friendly fraud now hits 83% of enterprise merchants. The 2026 data on first-party misuse, win rates, and what actually stops it.

Prime Domain Capital  ·  October 2026

The chargeback that cost a midsize electronics retailer $14,000 started with a doorbell camera. A customer ordered a high-end home theater system, signed for the delivery, installed it — and then called his bank to report the card stolen. The retailer had the delivery photo, the signature, and the IP address matching the billing address. The bank sided with the customer anyway.

That is friendly fraud. Visa calls it first-party misuse, a term that puts the focus where it belongs: the person filing the dispute received the goods. The word “friendly” undersells the damage. These chargebacks hit merchants as hard as criminal fraud, and they are harder to stop at checkout because the transaction looks completely normal when it happens.

The numbers nobody wants to say out loud

Start with the scale. Global card fraud losses hit $33.41 billion in 2024, according to the Nilson Report — and the United States accounts for 41.87% of those losses on just 26.31% of global volume. American merchants are the world’s most defrauded, by a wide margin.

The first-party slice is growing fastest. Visa puts friendly fraud at roughly 20% of all fraudulent disputes globally, rising to 30% for high-volume online merchants. Juniper Research expects it to climb from 22% of chargebacks in 2026 to 28% by 2031, with the transaction value growing from $8.1 billion to roughly $16 billion over that window. Datos Insights projects first-party fraud cases rising from 30.6 million in 2025 to 38.2 million by 2028.

The merchant-side numbers are starker. Chargebacks911’s 2026 Chargeback Field Report — built from survey responses from more than 250 merchants — found that 83.4% of enterprise merchants saw friendly fraud increase over the past three years. Across all merchant sizes, 73.7% reported an increase, and 74.4% now call it a moderate or significant concern. The 2026 LexisNexis Cybercrime Report found first-party fraud made up 38.3% of all reported global fraud in 2025, the leading category worldwide for a second consecutive year.

And the economics are brutal. LexisNexis puts the true cost of fraud at $4.61 for every $1 lost — counting fees, lost merchandise, operational overhead, and the labor of fighting disputes. The average chargeback value is $169.13. Merchants win only 44.6% of the disputes they fight, and the net recovery rate across all chargebacks — once second-cycle chargebacks are counted — is just 10.7%, per Chargebacks911. That means for every dollar a merchant loses to fraud, roughly ninety cents stays lost.

Why it keeps getting worse

Four shifts are driving dispute volume, and only one of them is criminal fraud, per Chargeback.io’s 2026 analysis. First, first-party fraud keeps climbing, and merchants feel it more than issuers do. Second, filing a dispute got easy — a few taps in a banking app, no phone call required. Third, card-not-present volume keeps growing, which widens exposure. Fourth, billing descriptors still confuse people, turning legitimate purchases into disputes.

The behavior repeats. About half of friendly fraudsters file again within 60 days of a win. Between 10% and 16% of consumers admit to disputing a charge they actually made. Subscription merchants face triple the friendly-fraud rate of everyone else — the “I forgot I subscribed” dispute is now a business model problem, not a customer-service one.

Then there is the regulatory vise. Visa’s Acquirer Monitoring Program now flags merchants at 150 basis points of dispute ratio in the US, Canada, EU, and Asia-Pacific, effective April 2026. Cross that threshold and the fines start. Merchants are being squeezed between rising first-party fraud they did not cause and network penalties for the resulting dispute ratios.

The honest counterargument

Here is what the fraud vendors will not tell you: some of what gets labeled “friendly fraud” is genuinely the merchant’s fault. Unclear billing descriptors drive roughly 40% of disputes. Opaque refund policies, dark-pattern subscriptions, and shipping delays that stretch past the customer’s patience all produce disputes that look like fraud in the data but read as legitimate grievances in the complaint.

The 2026 Chargebacks911 report itself notes that 38% of merchants say chargeback costs are now influencing the prices of their goods and services, up from 32.5% in the prior report. When honest customers absorb fraud costs through higher prices, some of those customers dispute more aggressively in return. The cycle feeds itself.

“Friendly fraud has moved from being a back-office inconvenience to a material business risk,” said Monica Eaton, founder and CEO of Chargebacks911. “It is influencing pricing, customer policies, staffing decisions and the economics of digital commerce.”

What actually works

The merchants pulling ahead treat dispute prevention as a data discipline, not a collections function. The playbook that shows results: Compelling evidence 3.0 and rapid dispute resolution to kill disputes before they become chargebacks; order validation and device fingerprinting at checkout for the criminal-fraud slice; clear billing descriptors and proactive delivery communication for the confusion slice; and representment only on winnable cases, because fighting everything burns labor on a 10.7% net recovery rate.

Dispute analytics — the unglamorous work of categorizing every chargeback by root cause and fixing the top three — reduces friendly fraud by roughly 45%, per industry data. The merchants still treating each chargeback as an individual billing problem are the ones paying the $100 billion tax.

The strategic question for 2026 is not whether friendly fraud will keep rising. Every forecaster says it will. It is whether merchants treat the dispute rate as a cost of doing business or as what it actually is: a pricing, policy, and data problem that compounds every quarter it goes unmanaged.
