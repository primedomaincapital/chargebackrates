Chargeback Insurance vs. Prevention Software: What Merchants Actually Get for the Fee

Insurance pays you back after you lose. Prevention stops the chargeback from ever posting. They are not two versions of the same product — they are two different jobs, and buying the wrong one is the standard mistake.

Prime Domain Capital Research  ·  November 19, 2026

The merchant had two quotes on his desk. One was for chargeback insurance: a policy that would pay him back when a chargeback was filed and lost. The other was for prevention software: a service that would stop chargebacks from being filed at all. The insurance quote was a percentage of volume. The prevention quote was a per-alert fee. Both salespeople called their product "chargeback protection." Both were wrong, in the same way, for opposite reasons. Insurance and prevention are not two versions of the same product. They are two different jobs, with different costs, different coverage, and different failure modes — and buying the wrong one is the standard mistake in this market, because the market is designed to make them look interchangeable.

The distinction starts with what each product actually does. Chargeback insurance is a policy or a processor add-on that pays the merchant back for the transaction amount and the fees after a chargeback is filed and lost. It does not stop the chargeback from happening. Prevention software — alerts, deflection, fraud scoring — stops the dispute from becoming a chargeback in the first place, or stops the fraudulent order from being placed at all. One moves the loss off the books after the fact. The other prevents the loss from being recorded. A merchant who buys insurance to fix a ratio problem will discover, too late, that the insurance does not fix the ratio. A merchant who buys prevention to recover lost revenue will discover that prevention does not recover anything. The products solve different problems, and the first step is naming the problem correctly.

What insurance actually covers

Chargeback insurance covers confirmed fraud and excludes almost everything else. The policy pays when a transaction is genuinely fraudulent — a stolen card, an account takeover — and the merchant loses the dispute. It does not cover friendly fraud, which is roughly 75 percent of all chargebacks: the legitimate cardholder who disputes a valid purchase, claims it never arrived, or simply does not recognize the charge. It does not cover merchant error, and it does not cover disputes once the merchant's chargeback ratio crosses the policy's limit. Verifi is blunt about it in its own insurance FAQ: most friendly fraud claims are not covered. The gap between what merchants think insurance covers and what it covers is the product's defining feature, and it is the reason the guarantee vendors do not publish prices.

The pricing reflects the gap. Only Stripe publishes a flat rate for its Chargeback Protection add-on: 0.4 percent per transaction, with coverage capped at $25,000 a year. PayPal and Shopify offer free but narrow coverage. Every guarantee vendor — Verifi, Chargebacks911, Signifyd, Riskified — quotes custom, because the fee depends on the merchant's fraud profile, vertical, and average ticket. A guarantee is a prevention vendor taking on liability inside a service, while insurance is a product bought on its own, and the distinction matters for the price: a guarantee vendor prices the risk of the orders it approves, and it will not approve the orders it cannot price. The merchant who wants a guarantee on a high-risk vertical will find the quote is either very expensive or not offered at all.

What prevention actually costs

Prevention is cheaper per incident, and the arithmetic is direct. Alert-based prevention — the pre-dispute alerts that let a merchant refund a transaction before the chargeback is filed — runs $15 to $29 per resolved dispute, billed per alert. Ethoca alerts, on the Mastercard side, run about $29; Verifi's RDR and CDRN alerts, on the Visa side, run about $15. The merchant pays per use, with no monthly minimum, and the alert buys 48 to 72 hours to resolve the dispute before it is logged. A chargeback that is never filed never counts against the ratio, and never triggers the fees. Compare that with the cost of a filed chargeback: the average US chargeback costs $110 per transaction, and the all-in cost — fees, operational time, and the network penalties — runs to $128, according to Mastercard and Javelin data from 2026. Stopping a dispute at the alert stage costs $20 to $30. Fighting it after it reaches the network costs $110 to $450. The gap is the whole business case for prevention, and it is the reason alerts are the first thing a merchant should buy.

Prevention has a real limit, and it is the limit the alert vendors do not advertise. Alerts only work on disputes the network catches, and alert coverage is not universal across card brands and banks. Ethoca covers roughly 95 percent of Mastercard but only about 30 percent of Visa, which is why no serious merchant runs it alone. Verifi's CDRN gives 72 hours to credit the cardholder on Visa and non-Visa disputes, and its network reaches Mastercard, Discover, and American Express as well as Visa — but coverage still moves with the card network and the processor together, and a merchant on Stripe or Shopify Payments will see lower coverage than a merchant on a traditional processor. Prevention stops the disputes it can see. It does nothing about the ones it cannot.

The third product nobody names

There is a third product in this market, and it is the one merchants actually need most often: representment — the work of fighting a chargeback after it has been filed, building the evidence file, and recovering the revenue. Representment does not improve the chargeback ratio, because a won dispute still counts in the ratio calculation, but it recovers cash, and cash is what the merchant is missing. The pricing is a success fee: Chargeflow charges 25 percent of what it recovers, with a 65 to 75 percent win rate; Justt runs an AI-native platform that automates the whole dispute workflow; Chargebacks911's 2026 Field Report puts its win rate at 44.6 percent with a net recovery of 10.7 percent. The spread between those numbers is the story: representment works, but it works on the cases it can win, and the cases it can win are the ones with evidence — delivery confirmation, customer communication, a clear paper trail. A merchant with no evidence has nothing to represent, and no software fixes that.

The fraud-prevention layer sits underneath all three. 3D Secure 2 shifts fraud dispute liability to the issuer on authenticated transactions, and merchants who implement it see a 50 to 80 percent reduction in fraud chargebacks on authenticated transactions, at $0 to 5 cents per authentication. Fraud scoring from Kount, Sift, or Signifyd runs $500 to $2,000 a month for enterprise merchants, and it is the layer that decides which orders to accept in the first place. The stack has three layers — prevention at the transaction, alerts before the filing, representment after it — and each solves a different problem. The standard mistake is buying one layer to fix a problem in another. A merchant buys alerts to fix a ratio problem that alerts only partly fix, or buys representment to stop disputes that have not been filed yet. The vendors do not correct the mistake, because the mistake is the sale.

What to buy, in what order

The order is the decision. First, 3D Secure 2 and fraud scoring, because they stop the fraudulent orders before they are placed, and they are cheap. Second, alerts — Ethoca and Verifi together, because neither covers the whole market alone — because they stop the dispute from being logged, and the ratio is the number that can close the account. Third, representment, because it recovers the revenue that prevention could not save. Insurance last, and only for the risk that remains after the first three layers: the confirmed-fraud losses on orders that were approved in good faith and still charged back. A merchant who buys insurance first has bought the most expensive product for the narrowest risk, and has left the ratio exposed.

One number governs the whole decision, and it is the number the article has to state carefully. Under Visa's VAMP program, the merchant excessive band sits at 1.5 percent, with a 1,500-dispute minimum, effective April 1, 2026. A lower figure — 0.9 percent — circulates in some vendor roundups and comparison pages, and it is disputed: it does not match the program thresholds as published, and this site's market-data page carries the 1.5 percent figure with the dispute noted. The difference matters because the threshold is the line between a monitoring program and an account closure, and a merchant who plans around the wrong number plans around the wrong risk. Use 1.5 percent. Treat 0.9 percent as unverified vendor material, not as a program rule.

The counterargument is that prevention is not free, either — and the merchants who refund every alert are surrendering winnable revenue. An alert resolved by refund is a sale given back, and a merchant who refunds every alert to protect the ratio is trading revenue for a number. The right policy is selective: refund the alerts where the evidence is weak, fight the ones where it is strong, and let the ratio be the constraint, not the objective. Insurance has the same problem in reverse: a merchant who buys a guarantee and stops caring about fraud will find the guarantee repriced or withdrawn, because the vendor is not an insurer of last resort. It is a counterparty, and counterparties reprice risk. The product that works is the one the merchant manages, and the management is the work. No vendor does it for you.
