What Is a Good Chargeback Rate by Industry in 2026?

Travel merchants run 0.89% to 2.00%. B2B SaaS runs 0.15%. The vendor tables disagree by three times — and the only number that can close your account is the one the networks publish as a threshold.

Prime Domain Capital Research  ·  November 19, 2026

The spreadsheet said 1.1%. The head of payments at a midsize online travel agency stared at it in February 2026 and felt the particular dread of a number she could not interpret. A year earlier, 1.1% would have been a shrug. Now Visa’s excessive line sat at 1.5%, her acquirer had started asking questions well below that, and a vendor benchmark table said travel merchants run anywhere from 0.89% to 2.00%. Was she safe, or three bad weeks from a monitoring program? That is the honest condition of chargeback benchmarking in 2026: everyone quotes a number, no two tables agree, and the only number that can close your account is one the networks publish as a threshold, not as an average.

Every merchant asks sooner or later: what is a good chargeback rate for a business like mine? The answer the merchant wants — a single average for their industry — does not exist in any form the card networks will stand behind. Visa and Mastercard do not publish industry-level chargeback rates. What exists instead is a shelf of vendor tables, each built from a different sample, definition, and commercial interest. This article publishes those bands with the disagreements left in, because the disagreement is the most useful fact in the subject. A merchant who knows the tables disagree by three times will use them wisely.

The bands, as the vendors report them

Start with the broadest compilation. Eightx’s 2026 benchmark, compiled from published pages at Chargebacks911, ClearlyPayments, and 2Accept, puts general card-not-present e-commerce at 0.6% to 1.0%, with the all-industry average near 0.65%. Retail and apparel sit at 0.52% to 1.00%. The hotter categories climb from there: subscriptions 0.90% to 1.20%, health and wellness 0.86% to 1.00%, digital goods 0.66% to 1.50%, travel and hospitality 0.89% to 2.00%, and electronics and CBD 1.00% to 2.00%. Read the range, not the midpoint: within one vertical, the offer type can move a merchant a full band.

A second table, from SeamlessChex in April 2026, built on Chargeflow’s 2025 statistics and Sift’s Q4 2024 Digital Trust Index, tells a similar story at lower levels. Travel and hospitality average 0.916%, e-commerce and online retail 0.47%, digital goods and subscriptions 0.54%, and card-present retail 0.50%. The year-over-year changes are the alarming part: travel up 816%, e-commerce up 222%, digital goods and subscriptions up 59%. The same table prices the dispute itself: the average disputed amount runs $120 in travel and hospitality, $84 in e-commerce, and $77 in digital goods and subscriptions.

A third set, attributed to the Merchant Risk Council and compiled by ChargebackStop in 2026, runs hotter still: digital goods and subscriptions at roughly 1.85%, travel and hospitality at 1.65%, e-commerce retail at 0.95%, food delivery at 0.80%, professional services at 0.40%, and B2B SaaS at 0.15%. Chargeback.io’s August 2026 breakdown lands in between: restaurants 0.12%, card-present retail 0.19%, health and beauty 0.34%, software and SaaS 0.66%, travel 0.89%, education 1.02%. ChargeMate’s 2026 guide adds the volume: United States chargebacks are projected to reach 146 million transactions worth $15.3 billion in 2026, with the average e-commerce merchant at roughly 0.95% — one transaction in 105. Global volume was 238 million transactions in 2023, projected to reach 337 million by 2026, per the Merchant Risk Council.

Three tables, one vertical, three different answers. Digital goods runs 0.54% in one and 1.85% in another. That gap is the story.

Why the tables disagree by three times

The disagreement is not sloppy work. It is arithmetic with different inputs. First, the numerator: some tables count filed chargebacks only, others count fraud reports plus disputes — the Visa VAMP definition, and a much larger number. Second, the denominator: disputes divided by transactions is the network convention, but some vendors divide by dollars, and a merchant selling $2,000 televisions looks nothing like one selling $9 subscriptions under a dollar-based rate. Third, the stage: alerts resolved before filing never become chargebacks, so a table built from a vendor with alert-network coverage shows lower rates than one built from raw issuer data. Fourth, the vertical boundaries: one table’s “digital goods” is another’s “subscriptions.” Fifth, the sample: enterprise merchants with dedicated risk teams dispute differently than the long tail of small sellers, and a table built from one vendor’s clients describes that vendor’s clients, not the market.

None of that makes the tables useless. It makes them conditional. A band is a weather report, not a speed limit: useful for knowing whether you are in a storm, useless for knowing whether you will be fined. The fine depends on thresholds, and those numbers are not in dispute.

Why the same rate is safe in one industry and a warning in another

Under Visa’s Acquirer Monitoring Program, VAMP, a merchant crosses into the Above Standard tier at a 0.9% combined fraud-and-dispute ratio and into the Excessive tier at 1.5%, with at least 1,500 combined fraud reports and disputes in the month; the excessive line was cut from 2.2% on April 1, 2026. Acquirers face their own tiers at 0.5% (Above Standard) and 0.7% (Excessive), and Visa charges $4 per flagged transaction in the Above Standard tier and $8 in the Excessive tier. Those are the April 2026 figures, as reported by Evidora, Forter, and Chargebacks911. One caution: two current guides from ThePaymentsEdge in 2026 argue the 0.9% merchant tier traces to the retired VDMP and VFMP era and does not appear in Visa’s published fact sheet, which prints only the 1.5% merchant line. Confirm your tier with your acquirer rather than assuming the table. Mastercard’s Excessive Chargeback Program triggers at a 1.5% ratio with 100 or more chargebacks in a month, and its High-Excessive tier starts at 3.0% with 300 or more.

Put a merchant on each side of those lines. A travel merchant at 1.4% is one bad month from Visa’s 1.5% line, already past its acquirer’s 0.7% line, and losing an average of $120 per dispute, per Mastercard’s 2025 data with Datos Insights — against $99 in high-risk categories, $84 in retail, $77 in digital goods, and $69 in subscriptions. A B2B SaaS merchant at the same 1.4% would be an outlier in a vertical averaging 0.15%, failing at a business with every structural advantage: contracted customers, deliberate purchases, transparent billing. The travel merchant’s 1.4% is a normal cost of selling cancellable, high-ticket, card-not-present inventory. The SaaS merchant’s 1.4% is a broken billing flow. Same number, opposite diagnosis.

The fixed cost sharpens the difference. Mastercard’s 2026 research with Javelin puts the all-in cost of a single chargeback at $128 — $82 of internal staff and systems cost plus $46 of third-party fees — and for merchants above $1 billion in revenue, the internal cost alone runs to $151.88 per dispute. At a $69 average subscription dispute, fighting costs more than the sale before the representment letter is written. At a $120 travel dispute, the same $128 is painful but no longer absurd. Stripe’s published guidance, cited in the 2026 benchmark literature, is blunter: any rate above 1% draws scrutiny from acquirers and card networks. The win rate does not rescue the math: merchants win only 44.6% of the disputes they fight, and net recovery across all chargebacks, once second-cycle chargebacks are counted, is just 10.7%, according to Chargebacks911’s 2026 Chargeback Field Report, built from more than 250 merchants. The report also found that 83.4% of enterprise merchants saw friendly fraud in the period. LexisNexis puts the true cost of fraud at $4.61 for every $1 lost, and Datos Insights, via Mastercard, puts the global impact of chargebacks at $36.9 billion in 2026, rising to $46.1 billion by 2029.

The honest counterargument

There is a serious case for ignoring every table in this article. Benchmarks invite complacency where complacency is dangerous: a travel merchant at 1.4% who reads that travel runs to 2.00% feels safe, while Visa’s line is 1.5% and the acquirer’s is 0.7% — the table told the merchant the wrong thing to feel good about. A subscription merchant at 0.8% who reads that subscriptions run to 1.20% relaxes while its own trend rises and its acquirer’s portfolio runs hot. The tables also flatter vendors: a prevention seller quoting the $36.9 billion global figure as proof every merchant needs its product is selling against a number that describes the whole market, not the buyer’s exposure. This site’s own market-data page excludes vendor rate tables for exactly this reason, stating that the networks do not publish industry-level rates and vendor tables use incompatible definitions. This article publishes the bands anyway, with the disagreement shown, because a merchant who sees three conflicting tables asks better questions than one who has seen none.

The counterargument has a second edge: a low rate is not a clean bill of health. A merchant at 0.3% whose acquirer’s portfolio breaches 0.7% can still lose its account, because Visa fines the acquirer on the portfolio and the acquirer protects itself by terminating its worst merchants first — judged inside the portfolio, not against the market. The line that terminates accounts is often the acquirer’s internal one, and nobody publishes that.

How to read your own number

Three yardsticks, in order. First, the network lines: your VAMP ratio against 0.9% and 1.5%, your Mastercard ratio against 1.5% with 100 chargebacks a month, and your acquirer’s standing against 0.5% and 0.7%. If you do not know your VAMP ratio, you do not know your exposure; track it monthly, and remember the denominator is last month’s settled card-not-present volume, so a seasonal sales dip can push the ratio up while dispute counts fall. Second, your vertical’s reported range — not as a target but as context: a retail merchant at 0.9% sits at the top of its band and should act, while a travel merchant at 0.9% sits at the bottom of its band and should still watch the network lines. Third, your own trend: a ratio rising quarter over quarter on flat volume is the alarm that matters most, and it fires months before any threshold does.

Then fix the numerator at its sources. Unclear billing descriptors drive roughly 40% of disputes, per industry data cited in this site’s VAMP analysis, and opaque refund policies and trial-to-paid conversions produce the friendly fraud that now dominates the count. Categorize every chargeback by root cause and fix the top three. The merchants staying under the line treat the dispute ratio as a metric they own, not a report they receive — and they know no benchmark table will save them. The ratio will.
