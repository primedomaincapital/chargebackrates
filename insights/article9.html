How Long Does a Chargeback Dispute Take to Resolve?

The funds leave in days. The verdict arrives in 30 to 90 days — or 240. Three clocks govern every chargeback, and the one that binds is rarely the one in the rulebook.

Prime Domain Capital Research  ·  November 19, 2026

The money left on a Tuesday. A home-goods merchant in Ohio — forty orders a day, average ticket $180 — watched a $2,300 sectional sofa order reverse itself in March 2026. The cardholder’s bank pulled the funds, issued provisional credit to the customer, and sent a notice giving the merchant thirty days to answer. The merchant answered in nine; the decision arrived in late May — eighty-two days after the sale. That gap — money gone in days, verdict in months — is the timeline. Ask how long a chargeback takes and you get three answers, because three clocks are running.

For a dispute that never leaves the first round, the answer is thirty to ninety days from filing to final decision. The long answer runs past eight months. The difference is which stages the case passes through, which network governs it, and whether the processor gave the merchant the full deadline or kept half of it.

Three clocks, not one

The first clock belongs to the cardholder, and it is the longest. Under Visa and Mastercard rules, a cardholder generally has 120 days from the transaction date to file a dispute, and for some cases — delayed delivery, services delivered long after purchase — the window stretches to 540 days. Visa’s Core Rules, October 24, 2026 edition, set the same 120-day window with a 540-day maximum for services-not-received disputes. American law adds its own frame: under the Fair Credit Billing Act, a cardholder must send written notice within 60 days of the statement date, and the issuer must resolve the matter within two billing cycles, at most 90 days.

The second clock belongs to the merchant, and it is the shortest. Once a chargeback is filed, the window to answer — representment, the submission of evidence that the transaction was legitimate — runs 30 calendar days at Visa and 45 calendar days at Mastercard, counted from the chargeback notice, with American Express at 20 days and Discover at 20 to 45 days depending on reason code. Those are the network windows per ChargebackClock, November 2026. The third clock belongs to the money: the funds leave within days, the cardholder gets provisional credit almost immediately, and if the merchant wins, the money comes back weeks or months later — minus the processor’s dispute fee, which never comes back.

The lifecycle, stage by stage

A chargeback moves through six stages, and most cases stop before the last two: filing, issuer review and reason code, merchant notification, representment, issuer review of the evidence, and — if either side refuses the result — pre-arbitration and then arbitration, where the network rules and the decision is binding. The published stage timings, compiled by WinningChargebacks in 2026: dispute to merchant notification takes 5 to 10 days at Visa, 5 to 15 at Mastercard, 3 to 7 at American Express. Issuer review takes 15 to 30 days at Visa and 15 to 45 at Mastercard. Pre-arbitration takes 30 days at Visa and 45 at Mastercard; arbitration takes 30 to 60 days at Visa and 30 to 90 at Mastercard. The totals: 110 to 160 days at Visa and 140 to 240 days at Mastercard when a case runs the full course, against 38 to 57 days at American Express, which has no arbitration stage.

Those are network maximums; the processor sets the real deadline. Stripe’s documentation, as compiled by ChargebackClock, gives merchants “usually 7 to 21 days, depending on the card network” to respond, with the issuer then usually taking 60 to 75 days to decide and the full lifecycle running two to three months. Adyen publishes the most granular table in the industry: from July 21, 2025, Visa disputes opened on payments processed locally in the United States and Canada carry a 9-day response window, and 18 days elsewhere; Mastercard allows 40 days; American Express 14; Discover 25. A merchant planning on Visa’s thirty days while processing through a provider that allows nine will miss the deadline with three weeks to spare.

The deadline that actually binds

The network deadline runs to the acquirer, and the acquirer keeps part of it for review, formatting, and forwarding. The date printed on the merchant’s own case notice is the one that binds, and it is days — sometimes weeks — earlier than the network figure. Every window is counted in calendar days from the notice date, not business days. Miss the representment deadline and the case ends by default: the dispute is decided in the cardholder’s favor with no evidence review, and the chargeback stands regardless of how strong the evidence was. The fix is unglamorous: a daily queue, a named owner, and evidence assembled the day the notice arrives.

The later stages have their own clocks, and they restart rather than continue. If the issuer rejects the representment, pre-arbitration gives the acquirer a further window: 30 days at Visa from the representment decision, and 45 days at Mastercard in the standard published table — though ChargebackClock’s November 2026 guide puts Mastercard’s window at 8 to 30 days depending on reason code, a disagreement to resolve with your acquirer first. Arbitration filing runs 10 days from pre-arbitration at Visa and 45 days at Mastercard. The decision takes about 45 days at Visa and 60 to 90 days at Mastercard, per Adyen’s guidelines via Chargeflow, November 2026. The fees change the decision, not just the cost: Visa charges $600 to whichever side loses, raised from $500 on April 1, 2025, and Mastercard’s losing-party fee is $575 ($25 Mastercom, $200 filing, $350 administration). Stripe does not support arbitration at all.

The shortcut: seventy-two hours instead of ninety days

There is one legitimate way to cut the timeline to almost nothing, and it happens before the chargeback exists. Pre-dispute alert networks — Ethoca, owned by Mastercard, and Verifi, owned by Visa, both acquired in 2019 — notify the merchant when a cardholder contacts their bank, giving the merchant 24 to 72 hours to refund before the formal chargeback is filed. Verifi’s CDRN product gives 72 hours for manual review; its Rapid Dispute Resolution product resolves qualifying disputes automatically under pre-set rules. Ethoca alerts carry a 48-to-72-hour window in most guides, 24 to 48 hours in others. Alerts cost $15 to $40 each, against a chargeback that costs $128 all-in, per Mastercard’s 2026 research with Javelin. American Express runs its own version, the ADR program, with an 8-day window.

That funnel is why alerts matter more than representment. Mastercard’s State of Chargebacks research, as reported by ChargePay, found that only 8% of cases are resolved at the early pre-dispute stage, issuers discard another 18%, and a full 74% escalate into formal chargebacks — and only 54% of merchants fight back with representment at all. The evidence stage changed recently: Visa’s Compelling Evidence 3.0 framework lets merchants defeat card-absent fraud disputes, reason code 10.4, with two prior undisputed purchases matched on IP address or device, aged 120 to 365 days. An update effective October 24, 2026 widened the pool so prior purchases from other merchants using the same card or token can count, though only purchases the merchant’s own acquirer processed, as reported in payments trade coverage that month — and the evidence has to be captured at checkout, 120 days before the dispute arrives.

The funds leave in days. The verdict arrives in months. The merchant finances the whole interval — and pays $128 for the privilege.

The honest counterargument

Speed is the wrong goal, and a merchant that optimizes for it will lose money faster. The $128 all-in cost of fighting exceeds the value of the average subscription dispute — $69, per Mastercard’s 2025 data with Datos Insights — so a subscription merchant that fights every dispute loses money even when it wins. A $600 arbitration fee on a $47 dispute is not a close call; it is a refund. Merchants win only 44.6% of the disputes they fight, and net recovery across all chargebacks, once second-cycle chargebacks are counted, is just 10.7%, according to Chargebacks911’s 2026 Chargeback Field Report. Refunding inside the alert window costs the sale but never enters the VAMP numerator — refunds are not disputes — while a fought-and-lost chargeback counts in full. The fastest resolution is the dispute that never becomes one.

The counterargument cuts the other way too. Provisional credit and the 120-day filing window exist because merchants used to stall: a customer who waited weeks for a refund that never came had no other lever. A merchant that refunds quickly and prints a descriptor a customer recognizes will see fewer disputes reach the timeline at all. ChargePay reports that merchants who respond within the first ten days win 54% of their disputes, against the 44.6% average — a single-vendor figure, but one that points the same direction as everything else here: the timeline rewards the merchant who moves first.

What the calendar should change

Four practices follow from the clocks. First, build the evidence packet at checkout: IP address, device identifier, delivery confirmation, and customer messages, retained for at least 365 days — Compelling Evidence 3.0 reaches back 120 to 365 days and the filing window reaches back 540. Second, calendar the processor’s deadline, not the network’s, and submit evidence in the first ten days. Third, set a fight-or-refund line by ticket size and reason code, and let the alert networks take the small cases automatically. Fourth, watch the ratio: under VAMP the merchant excessive line is 1.5% with a 1,500-event minimum, the acquirer lines are 0.5% and 0.7%, and the fees run $4 and $8. A dispute that takes 160 days to resolve still counts in the month it was filed.

The timeline is a schedule with three clocks, published deadlines, and a fee at every stage. The merchants who manage it treat the dispute the way they treat any other payable: dated, owned, and settled on the earliest deadline, not the latest. The ones who wait for the network’s clock will keep learning the Ohio merchant’s lesson — the money was gone in March, the answer came in May, and the only part of the schedule they controlled was the nine days in between.
