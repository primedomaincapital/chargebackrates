VAMP’s New Math: The 150-Basis-Point Line Every Merchant Now Lives Under

Visa cut its excessive-dispute threshold to 1.5% on April 1, 2026 — and started charging $8 per disputed transaction. The merchants who treated disputes as a cost center just acquired a new regulator.

Prime Domain Capital  ·  November 2026

The statement arrived in the first week of March 2026, and the finance director of a midsize subscription merchant read it twice. Her company’s Visa dispute ratio was 1.6%, built from about 1,800 fraud reports and disputes in the month. Under the rules she had operated under for years, 1.6% was fine — the old excessive line sat at 2.2%. Under the rules taking effect on April 1, 2026, she was already over it. Nothing about her business had changed. The line had moved underneath her.

That is the Visa Acquirer Monitoring Program, VAMP, and in 2026 it stopped being a compliance footnote and became a pricing event. On June 1, 2025, Visa folded its two legacy merchant programs — the Visa Dispute Monitoring Program and the Visa Fraud Monitoring Program — into a single framework. The VAMP ratio counts fraud reports (TC40) plus all disputes (TC15), fraud and non-fraud alike, divided by settled card-not-present transactions. The advisory period ended on September 30, 2025, enforcement began on October 1, 2025, and on April 1, 2026 the merchant excessive threshold in the US, Canada, Europe, and Asia-Pacific dropped from 220 basis points to 150 — with a minimum of 1,500 fraud and dispute events a month to qualify. Ravelin’s VAMP guide tracks each of those steps, and the direction is one way: tighter, and sooner than most merchants planned for.

The threshold is only half the story. The other half is the invoice. Once a merchant is classed as excessive, Visa’s enforcement fee runs at $8 per card-not-present fraud or disputed transaction, in effect since October 2025. Acquirers have their own bands: above standard from 0.5% to 0.7% costs $4 per dispute, excessive above 0.7% costs $8 — and acquirers pass those fees straight through to the merchants whose activity caused them. Authsignal’s breakdown of the VAMP fee schedule makes the mechanism plain: the network fines the acquirer, the acquirer bills the merchant, and the merchant discovers the program through its statement, not through a warning letter.

There is a second tripwire most merchants have never heard of. VAMP also watches enumeration — card-testing attacks, where stolen card numbers are probed with small authorizations. If more than 20% of a merchant’s authorizations are enumerated attempts, and the count passes roughly 300,000 in a month, enforcement can apply at $25,000 a month. TabaPay’s network monitoring documentation lays out the ratio and the fee. A merchant can now enter a monitoring program without a single customer dispute, simply because criminals chose its checkout as a testing ground.

Mastercard runs a parallel system with its own numbers. Its Excessive Chargeback Program triggers at 1.0% chargebacks with at least 100 chargebacks a month, and the high-excessive tier at 1.5% with 300 or more. The Finrate’s 2026 comparison of the two networks’ programs shows the same shape on both sides: count-based thresholds, monthly minimums, and escalation that ends in losing the ability to process cards. Two scoreboards, one direction.

Visa stopped asking whether a merchant’s disputes were fraud. It started asking whether there were too many of them. That is a different question, and most merchants are still answering the wrong one.

Why the old playbook can’t move the number

The instinctive response — fight every chargeback harder — fails on arithmetic. Merchants win only 44.6% of the disputes they fight, and the net recovery rate across all chargebacks, once second-cycle chargebacks are counted, is just 10.7%, according to Chargebacks911’s 2026 Chargeback Field Report. LexisNexis puts the true cost of fraud at $4.61 for every $1 lost. Representment is a recovery tool. VAMP is a count problem. Winning 45% of your disputes still leaves 100% of them in the numerator.

That is the conceptual shift merchants keep missing. Under the old VDMP, the question was chargebacks as a share of sales, and the fix was better evidence. Under VAMP, fraud reports count even when no dispute is ever filed, non-fraud disputes count alongside fraud ones, and the denominator is last month’s settled card-not-present volume. A merchant whose sales dip for a seasonal reason can watch its ratio rise while its dispute count falls. The ratio is a moving target with a lagging denominator, and it punishes exactly the merchants least able to staff a response.

The honest counterargument

Not all of this is the network’s doing. Some of what VAMP catches is genuinely the merchant’s fault: unclear billing descriptors drive roughly 40% of disputes, and opaque refund policies and dark-pattern subscriptions produce disputes that read as fraud in the data and as legitimate grievances in the complaint. A merchant tripping the 150-basis-point line because its descriptor is cryptic is not a victim of the threshold. It is a victim of its own checkout page.

The program also has a genuine small-merchant shield. The 1,500-event monthly minimum means a small business with a terrible ratio but a few hundred disputes never enters VAMP at all. And the pressure lands first on acquirers, whose own 0.5% and 0.7% bands bite before the merchant threshold does — which means the acquirer, not Visa, is usually the one that calls. That intermediation is a mercy and a risk at once: a good acquirer helps the merchant fix the ratio, and a squeezed one simply terminates the account to protect its own standing.

What actually moves the ratio

The merchants staying under the line treat the VAMP ratio as a metric they own, not a report they receive. They track it monthly, the way they track a credit score, and they attack the numerator at its sources: Compelling Evidence 3.0 and rapid dispute resolution to kill disputes before they become chargebacks; clear billing descriptors and proactive delivery communication for the confusion slice; rate limiting and bot defenses at checkout for the enumeration slice; and dispute analytics that categorize every chargeback by root cause and fix the top three. The unglamorous work of categorizing disputes reduces friendly fraud by roughly 45%, per industry data — and under VAMP, that reduction now shows up directly in the number that decides whether you keep processing.

The strategic question for the rest of 2026 is not whether the threshold moves again. It just did, and the enforcement machinery is live. It is whether merchants start managing the dispute ratio as a compliance metric with a hard ceiling — because that is what Visa built. The merchants who do will keep their processing. The ones who keep treating disputes as a billing problem will discover, one statement at a time, that the billing problem now has a regulator.
